The Attention Ledger

An operating system for a ten-person company, built on one premise: the scarce resource is not time, money, or headcount. It is noticing — and in a small company, noticing is nobody's job.

Status running on live data Surface Outlook · Slack · Zoom · Finaloop · Shopify · Klaviyo Sends nothing ever, on anyone's behalf

What this optimizes, and what it refuses to count

Most systems that touch a company's work optimize for throughput — messages handled, tasks created, workflows fired. Ask them how they are doing and they will tell you how much they did.

This one answers a different question: how much useful progress the company made per unit of its people's attention, the founder's most of all. That single choice determines every design decision downstream, and it has one corollary worth stating plainly, because it rules out most of what gets built in this category:

A system that produces more tasks has made things worse.

Under that rule, an automation that files forty emails into a queue is a cost, not a feature. The only things that earn a place are the ones that change what a person does in the next hour: a decision that is quietly blocking four people, a promise nobody wrote down, a number that means something different from what it appears to mean.

Capacity is not the problem

In a company of ten, everybody already knows they are busy. What actually fails is narrower and more specific — and none of it shows up in anyone's inbox as a thing to do:

  • A supplier said "I'll follow up" and never gave a date. Six weeks later, nobody has thought about it since. Undated promises are the ones that vanish, precisely because there is nothing to be late against.
  • A decision only the founder can make is sitting behind four people's work — and none of them said so, because each of them is only blocked on their own piece.
  • Something got finished and nothing was ever closed, so it keeps occupying a slot in everyone's head and a line in every list.
  • Ad spend rose 23% and sessions did not move, but the spend lives in the accounting system and the sessions live in the storefront, and nobody had both open on the same day.
  • A five-minute approval that would have freed a colleague's entire day got done at 4:30pm.

In a large company, noticing all this is somebody's job — an ops team, a chief of staff, a project manager. In a small one it is nobody's, which is exactly what makes it worth building rather than buying.

The part almost everyone skips

Reading a week of email and producing a ranked list is the easy half, and it is where most tools stop. The problem only shows up in month two: a system with no memory rebuilds the same queue every morning from the same evidence. Nothing can ever be finished, and nothing can be compared to yesterday.

Five rules that do most of the work

Ownership is six roles, not one

Real work has a primary owner, a project manager, a decision maker, an approver, collaborators, and very often an external counterparty. Collapsing all of that into a single assignee field is where most tools lose the plot.

The consequence that matters: needing the founder is not the same as being the founder's job. A production slip needs Adi's decision and Mike's execution. Assign it to Adi and you have just moved a week of operations onto the wrong desk — quietly, and with the system's blessing.

Attention is priced by what it costs, not by what is at stake

Approving a $50,000 production run and approving an Instagram post both take about five minutes. Ordering a day by value at stake would therefore get it systematically wrong.

So each item is priced by its mode — decide, approve, delegate, do, follow up — and the plan is budgeted against the hours actually left. A plan that needs nine hours at 3pm is not a plan, and saying otherwise is how a daily tool stops being opened.

The value of unblocking someone decays through the day

Approving Chase's content at 9am buys a full day of his work. The identical approval at 4:30pm buys nothing until tomorrow.

So a five-minute approval that frees a colleague outranks an hour of the founder's own higher-scoring work in the morning — and stops outranking it by late afternoon. The order changes with the clock, which no static priority list can do.

A number is only true once its period is closed

The books close monthly, by the 10th. Until a month closes, its expense side is incomplete — bills not entered, transactions not categorized — so any profit figure drawn from it is fiction wearing the costume of fact.

Profit and expense conclusions therefore come only from closed periods, compared against the previous closed period, with the month named so that "now" is never assumed. The open month appears as revenue only, labelled with the date its books close. This one rule reversed the system's headline finding the first time it ran.

A change must clear the noise before it is reported

A weekly conversion rate moves several tenths of a point on nothing at all. A system that announces every wobble teaches its reader to ignore it — and then it is ignored on the day it matters.

So movements are converted into standard errors and anything under three sigma is reported as what it is: within chance. Restraint is the feature. A dashboard that never says "this is probably nothing" is not measuring, it is decorating.

Three refusals

Everything above is judgment, and judgment can be wrong. What makes the system safe to leave running is not its accuracy — it is what it declines to do when it is confident.

Never

Sends as you

Not one message leaves in anyone's name. When a supplier is overdue, the output is a draft, addressed to whoever owns the chasing, quoting what the supplier actually said. The system contributes the noticing — the part that fails — not the relationship.

Never

Closes what it cannot safely close

A task wrongly left open is visible and costs a moment to dismiss. One wrongly closed disappears, along with whatever it was worth. So inference closes only what is both confident and cheap to be wrong about; anything consequential becomes a question with the evidence quoted.

Never

Treats silence as completion

Work that stops appearing has usually either been finished with nobody saying so, or quietly died. Those need opposite responses. After three weeks of silence an item leaves the daily plan and is asked about once — a daily "is this still live?" is the same nagging in slower form.

Three times reality corrected it

Every rule above exists because the system got something wrong against real data first. These are the three that changed the architecture.

1 · The loss that was a profit

On its first real run the system led with a confident headline: the company was losing money and ad spend had exploded. It was reading the open month, whose expenses were half-entered. The closed months said close to the opposite.

What the open month showed
−$53k
Net, with ad spend apparently up 61%. Drawn from a month whose bills were not yet entered.
What the closed books said
+$34k
Profit improving, with spend up 23%. Nearly the opposite conclusion, from the same report.

The fix was not a better model. It was a rule about when a number is allowed to be believed — and it is the reason the finance panel now names the month it is talking about and shows the open one as revenue only.

2 · The status list that closed a whole project

Completion is inferred from evidence, because nobody at a ten-person company reliably closes tasks. Run against real Slack traffic, it read this and marked a four-marketplace listing project finished:

Slack · marketplace channel The product images were updated on the marketplaces. Walmart — waiting for approval, do you want me to list the 28ct stickpack? Faire — can we activate the 28ct stickpack in draft? TikTok — done. Amazon — waiting for image processing.

One line out of four was finished, and that line closed everything. The rule that came out of it: any unfinished marker anywhere in a message settles it. That is a progress report, not a completion, however confident the rest of it sounds. A second rule fell out of the same investigation — a message cannot finish the work it asked for, since overlapping windows mean Monday's request gets read again on Tuesday and was closing the task it had opened.

3 · The promise with no date

The follow-up engine had been written months earlier and had never once fired. It measured lateness against a due date, and the most common promise in real business correspondence has no date at all:

Email · media partner I will look into these hosts and follow up.

Nothing to be late against, so it was never chased — which means the promises being dropped were exactly the ones the system was structurally blind to. With memory, the clock starts when we first heard it. Replayed against the real inbox, that one change surfaced the commitment at ten business days outstanding, quoting the partner's own words back to whoever has to send the nudge.

Where this transfers

The temptation is to say "any small business", which is both true and useless. There is a sharper predictor, and it is not industry or headcount:

How many outside parties can make you a promise?

Every one of them is a place a ball gets dropped, and none of them are in your systems. A company with three suppliers and one channel does not need this. A company with a contract manufacturer, a 3PL, two retail buyers, a media agency, a marketplace and a lab has a dropped-ball surface no individual can hold in their head — and no software they own is watching it, because the evidence lives in threads.

Count the outside parties who owed you something last month.

Over eight, and nobody's job is tracking them? Strong fit. Under four? Buy a shared spreadsheet.

If your two busiest people took a week off, how much would stop?

Most of it? Attention is your binding constraint, and everything here is aimed at it.

Where would a new hire look to find out what is outstanding?

"Read my email and ask around" means you have evidence but no state — which is the exact gap the ledger fills.

Business shapeFitWhat carries the value
Consumer brand, 5–50 people Strong The original case. Contract manufacturers, 3PLs, retail buyers, agencies and marketplaces all making commitments by email, with a founder as the decision bottleneck and real numbers scattered across three systems.
Agency or professional services firm Strong Commitments are the product, and "who owns this client thread" is genuinely ambiguous rather than merely undocumented. The follow-up engine is worth more here than anywhere else.
B2B with long implementation cycles Strong Multi-party, slow-moving work where a dropped ball is expensive and stays invisible for weeks. Deadline-aware re-ranking matters most when the gap between cause and consequence is long.
Import/export, brokerage, franchise ops Strong Enormous counterparty surface, almost everything by email, and approval classes that genuinely differ by transaction rather than by policy theatre.
Regulated small operation (clinic, lab, food) Partial Approval routing and the never-auto-send rule become the point rather than a footnote — but the finance and funnel layers are mostly irrelevant, so roughly half the system is inert.
Work already in a system of record people update Poor If your tracker is accurate, you do not need inference over evidence — you need a better view of what you already have. This whole architecture exists because the truth lives in threads.
High-volume repetitive operations Poor Support queues, claims, ticketed fulfilment. The economics are per-ticket, so throughput automation is the right tool. Allocating attention optimizes a variable that is not binding.
Large organization with a real org chart Poor A learned capability graph adds nothing over an accurate directory, and an ops function already does the noticing. The gap this fills is specifically the gap where nobody's job is to notice.
Solo operator Poor No delegation surface, so ownership routing, approval classes and the unblocking rule are all inert. What remains is a to-do list with unusually good manners.

What moves to a new company, and what gets rebuilt

The honest split is unusually clean, because the business-specific parts were deliberately pushed into configuration rather than code.

Transfers unchanged

  • The ledger: identity across runs, carry-forward, dormancy
  • Completion detection and its refusals
  • Follow-up cadence, escalation and the undated-promise clock
  • Deduplication across email, chat and calls
  • Approval classes and the never-auto-send boundary
  • The day plan, including the decay rule
  • Statistical gating on every reported movement

Rebuilt per business

  • The capability graph — who is actually good at what
  • Business areas and their typical decision makers
  • Which counterparties exist and who owns each relationship
  • Connectors, and which numbers are worth pulling
  • Finance rules: when the books close, what "true" means in them
  • Priority weights and what triggers an escalation

In practice that is ten configuration files against fifty-seven code modules. Standing this up somewhere new is a week of describing how a company actually works — not a rebuild.

It runs on real correspondence, real books and real storefront data today, and opens as a single page: what to do and in what order, everything outstanding, the numbers that matter, and a short list of things the company could choose to take on. Nothing in it is theoretical — every rule above was written after the system got the corresponding thing wrong.

The next three things are known and small: reading our own sent mail so the system finds the promises we made, days of inventory cover from the storefront, and blended customer acquisition cost on closed months — the one number that decides whether the growth plan works, which nothing currently computes because the spend and the customers live in different systems.

Figures cited are drawn from live company data and are illustrative of the mechanism rather than a financial statement. The system holds real correspondence; it is private by default and shares nothing without a deliberate action.
